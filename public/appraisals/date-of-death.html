Date-of-death appraisals for Los Angeles County homes

A date-of-death appraisal establishes what a home was worth on the day its owner died. For inherited Los Angeles County property, that figure becomes the heirs' new tax basis, anchors trust and estate accounting, and gives everyone involved a neutral number to work from, even when the appraisal is ordered months or years later.

Why the date matters more than today's price

Federal tax law resets the basis of inherited property to its fair market value on the date of death (Internal Revenue Code §1014). If a parent bought a Pasadena bungalow decades ago, the heirs' gain on a later sale is measured from the date-of-death value, not the original purchase price. A credible retrospective appraisal is how that number is documented.

Because the valuation looks backward, the analysis relies on comparable sales and market conditions that existed at the time, not on current listings. In a market that has moved quickly, the gap between a date-of-death value and today's value can be large in either direction.

Ordering one long after the fact

Many trustees and heirs first learn they need a date-of-death value when a CPA prepares a return or when the house is about to be sold. That is common and not a problem: a retrospective appraisal can be prepared for any past date for which sales data exists, and Los Angeles County's sales record runs deep.

California community property and a full step-up

When a married couple holds a home as community property, both halves generally receive a new basis at the first spouse's death, not only the decedent's half (IRC §1014(b)(6)). That makes the date-of-death value of a California home doubly important for a surviving spouse. Your CPA or estate attorney will confirm how title was held and how the rule applies to you.

What the report documents

Each report states the effective date, describes the property as of that date, presents the comparable sales relied on and the market conditions at the time, and explains the reasoning behind the final opinion of fair market value. It is prepared under the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP) and signed by a California Certified Residential Real Estate Appraiser, which is the support CPAs and estate attorneys expect to find in the file.

Questions about date-of-death appraisals

Can the appraisal use photos taken after the date of death?

Yes. Recent photos help us understand the home. Tell us about anything that changed after the date of death, such as cleanup, repairs, or remodeling, so the report reflects the property as it was on the effective date.

Is a date-of-death appraisal the same as the probate referee's value?

No. In a California probate, a court-appointed probate referee values real property for the estate inventory. Heirs and trustees often still want an independent date-of-death appraisal for income-tax basis records, trust administration, or decisions among family members; your attorney can advise how the two fit together.

What if the home sold shortly after the death?

A nearby-in-time, arm's-length sale of the home itself is meaningful evidence. The appraisal still analyzes the market as of the date of death and explains how the later sale relates to that value.
